# A — TOPIC STRATEGY AND ARTICLE IDEATION

Load this category only when the article task, affected runtime boundary, or release risk selects it. The directives below are exact authorized requirements. Record applicability and live evidence in the adopting repository; policy presence is not runtime verification.

- **A01.** Establish a site-specific editorial charter from the actual brand, audience, product, geography, language, tone, and business purpose. Every proposed article must advance that charter; do not invent a niche when configuration is missing.
- **A02.** Organize topic discovery around concrete reader jobs, decisions, frustrations, and questions. State the reader's starting situation and useful outcome for every candidate so search demand serves an identifiable human need.
- **A03.** Build an audience knowledge map distinguishing beginner, practitioner, and expert needs. Choose the required level deliberately, and avoid producing superficially different articles whose only distinction is an audience label.
- **A04.** Read the site's complete available inventory, including published articles, drafts, scheduled work, reserved topics, redirects, and canonical URLs. Record inventory coverage and disclose gaps before asserting an opportunity is new.
- **A05.** Research each opportunity using primary documentation, original research, public datasets, reputable specialist reporting, and relevant current developments. Search-engine results and keyword metrics guide discovery but cannot substitute for substantive subject research.
- **A06.** Before every article, research and produce exactly fifteen distinct candidate ideas within the site's editorial scope. These must represent meaningful questions or angles, not fifteen title variations around the same answer.
- **A07.** Give each candidate a structured record: working title, reader job, proposed angle, topic seed, intended search intent, audience, evidence leads, distinct contribution, inventory overlap, timeliness, likely format, and estimated production effort.
- **A08.** Apply an evidence-feasibility check before selecting a candidate. Prefer ideas with accessible, sufficiently authoritative material to support the promised analysis; downgrade ideas that depend on unverifiable claims or unavailable proprietary data.
- **A09.** Identify the original contribution of each idea: a defensible comparison, synthesis, worked example, decision framework, documented experiment, or new explanation. Do not promise experiments, interviews, first-hand experience, or proprietary findings that have not occurred.
- **A10.** Score relevance to both reader needs and legitimate business goals. Make the connection concrete, such as helping users evaluate a decision or use a product successfully, without turning every topic into a sales pitch.
- **A11.** Maintain a topical map connecting major subjects, supporting questions, existing articles, and uncovered reader needs. Use it to build coherent coverage instead of expanding into unrelated high-volume subjects solely to attract traffic.
- **A12.** Prioritize the next useful question inside a cluster, including prerequisites, comparisons, implementation obstacles, and advanced applications. Avoid mechanically generating every conceivable keyword permutation when it adds no distinct reader benefit.
- **A13.** Define the intended outcome behind each candidate's search intent. Distinguish learning, evaluating, troubleshooting, navigating, and purchasing situations, and verify that the planned article is an appropriate response to that situation.
- **A14.** Choose a format because it suits the question: explainer, tutorial, comparison, decision guide, case analysis, troubleshooting reference, research synthesis, or timely analysis. Format diversity is useful only when the underlying reader task differs.
- **A15.** Hand the selected candidate's actual subject and natural topic seed to the DataForSEO workflow. Keep the topic-to-seed relationship explicit so keyword discovery refines the article rather than silently replacing its purpose.
- **A16.** Use a configurable, documented editorial scoring rubric for reader value, originality, evidence feasibility, intent fit, topical relevance, attainable search opportunity, and timeliness. Label weights as editorial choices, never Google ranking factors or guarantees.
- **A17.** Separate eligibility gates from ranking scores. An attractive demand estimate cannot compensate for a duplicate angle, absent evidence, inappropriate brand fit, or an article promise the available research cannot fulfill.
- **A18.** Compare candidates against existing and planned content using semantic similarity, core questions, intended answer, audience, and intent. Title similarity and exact keyword matching alone are insufficient to identify duplicates reliably.
- **A19.** Resolve ambiguous overlap by comparing the actual reader decision and expected answer. Two pages can share terminology while serving different needs; conversely, differently worded titles may compete to answer the same question.
- **A20.** Select the highest-ranked eligible original candidate; move to the second or third when earlier candidates substantially overlap existing work. If all fifteen fail, research another set instead of manufacturing novelty or bypassing eligibility.
- **A21.** Prefer updating, expanding, or consolidating an existing article when it already owns the intended question and search intent. Recommend a new URL only when its contribution and audience need are materially distinct.
- **A22.** Treat topics already reserved by another article run as occupied during selection. Recheck the inventory immediately before finalizing the choice so three daily runs do not independently choose essentially the same opportunity.
- **A23.** Balance the topic portfolio across discovery, evaluation, practical application, troubleshooting, and deeper expertise in proportions suited to the site. Avoid selecting all daily articles from whichever intent happens to have the largest estimated volume.
- **A24.** Maintain an explicit balance between durable reference topics and genuinely timely developments. Newsworthiness requires an identifiable new development; changing a year in a headline does not make an old topic meaningfully fresh.
- **A25.** Use seasonal demand patterns, product cycles, and relevant events to prioritize lead time. Distinguish predictable recurring interest from a temporary spike, and explain when historical data is insufficient to support a seasonal conclusion.
- **A26.** Evaluate geographic and language-specific opportunities against actual audience needs and local evidence. Create separate local versions only when they materially change the answer, terminology, available products, rules, or reader decisions.
- **A27.** Consider whether a candidate supports useful visual explanation, comparison, calculation, or interaction. Prefer topics where such components clarify real concepts or evidence, rather than choosing an idea merely to fill a visual quota.
- **A28.** Consider production cost, research effort, available expertise, and likely useful shelf life when prioritizing ideas. High-effort ideas can justify investment when their distinctive reader value exceeds that of easier alternatives.
- **A29.** Maintain configurable editorial exclusions and escalation rules appropriate to the actual site, including unsupported subject areas and topics requiring qualified expertise. Apply these rules to candidate selection before research costs and production effort accumulate.
- **A30.** Preserve the fifteen-candidate slate, score rationale, evidence leads, overlap decisions, chosen topic, and rejected alternatives with each article brief. Make selection explainable and reusable without presenting speculative opportunity scores as measured outcomes.
